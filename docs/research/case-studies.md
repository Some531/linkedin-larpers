# Case studies: what actually happened

Real, documented disasters. Each entry covers what happened, what failed, the
CALD / hard-to-reach dimension, and the transferable lesson.

Confidence labels: **[inquiry]** = official inquiry or after-action report ·
**[peer-reviewed]** · **[reported]** = credible journalism or NGO report.
Full citations in [`sources.md`](sources.md).

---

## Australian cases

### 1. Northern Rivers / Lismore floods — NSW, February–March 2022 **[inquiry]**

**What happened.** Record flooding overwhelmed Lismore and the Northern Rivers. The
Wilsons River exceeded its previous record height, inundating homes that had never
flooded before.

**What failed.**
- The NSW Flood Inquiry found the risk of severe weather **was not adequately
  communicated** through SES flood bulletins, its website, Facebook, or other warning
  channels. Many residents **never received the evacuation SMS**.
- **Critical sensors were broken or wrong.** Creek and rainfall gauges feeding the
  official warning system were either non-functional or transmitting inaccurate data —
  while residents standing next to those creeks could see the flood coming.
- Emergency call capacity collapsed. One pregnant resident and her family called
  emergency services **35 times** and were not rescued.
- **More than 1,000 people were rescued by their own neighbours**, in private boats,
  after official channels did not respond. The community out-performed the system.
- Human Rights Watch concluded the response **failed to protect those most at risk**,
  including people with disability, older people, and low-income renters.
- The inquiry made **28 recommendations**, including overhauling Resilience NSW.

**Hard-to-reach dimension.** The failure was not only linguistic — it was structural.
The people worst served were those least visible to the system: renters, people
without cars, people not on the right Facebook page.

**Lesson.** Two independent failures compounded: the system did not know what was
happening (bad sensors), and the people who did know had no channel back in. A warning
system with no upstream path from residents is running half-blind. Meanwhile the actual
effective response network — neighbours with boats — was entirely informal and
unsupported.

### 2. Black Summer bushfires — Australia, 2019–20 **[peer-reviewed]**

**What happened.** The most severe fire season in modern Australian record: 33 direct
deaths, ~24 million hectares burnt, and smoke exposure across the eastern seaboard for
months.

**What failed (communication specifically).**
- Communication strategy focused almost entirely on **the fire front**, leaving a
  documented knowledge gap on **bushfire smoke health risk** — the hazard that affected
  vastly more people, for far longer.
- Research on caregivers found **language barriers** and the absence of child-friendly
  communication were specific, named obstacles, worst for **non-English-speaking
  minority groups**.
- CALD communities were disadvantaged by unfamiliarity with warning signs, limited
  knowledge of what to do, and not knowing which services to contact.

**Lesson.** "Warning" is not one message. Acute-hazard messaging (evacuate now) and
chronic-hazard messaging (smoke, water, heat, weeks of it) have different audiences and
different failure modes. The chronic side is systematically under-served, and it is
where CALD households — often in poorer housing, often in outdoor work — take the most
damage.

### 3. Victorian floods — October 2022 **[reported]**

**What happened.** Widespread riverine flooding across northern and central Victoria.

**What failed.**
- Migrant agricultural workers **were unaware of the danger until they were stranded on
  roofs**.
- Some workers were instructed by employers to stay on farms, relocate to factories
  without facilities, and **ignore road closures**. Temporary visa holders reported fear
  of visa cancellation if they disobeyed.
- Emergency information was disseminated by staff and volunteers who "**overwhelmingly
  speak only English**", which, in Amnesty International Australia's assessment,
  rendered radio, the **VicEmergency app, and even 000 effectively useless** for many.
- Recovery volunteers could not communicate past language and literacy barriers;
  translation of documents for insurance and compensation was a further barrier.
- No state-wide consistency — language support varied by municipality.

**Lesson.** This is the cleanest documented case of the brief's core claim. The
technology existed and worked. It was in one language, delivered by people who spoke
one language, and so it did not exist for the people at highest risk. It also shows the
**power dimension**: a worker on a temporary visa may receive and understand a warning
and still be unable to act on it. Warning design that ignores employer coercion and visa
precarity will not change behaviour.

### 4. Ex-Tropical Cyclone Alfred — South East Queensland, March 2025 **[reported]**

**What happened.** The first tropical cyclone to track onto the Brisbane region in
**over 50 years** (last comparable: 1974). Alfred crossed near Brisbane on 8 March 2025
after an erratic, slow approach, bringing destructive wind, a 12.3 m wave recorded off
the Gold Coast (highest in 38 years), and major flooding. Over **34,000 insurance claims
within four days**.

**What failed / what was hard.**
- The hazard was **novel to the population**. Effectively nobody under 50 in Brisbane
  had personally experienced a cyclone. Local risk knowledge — the thing the brief calls
  out as a CALD vulnerability — was low across the *entire* population, and lowest among
  recent arrivals.
- The long, uncertain approach (days of "it might turn") is exactly the profile that
  generates **warning fatigue** and premature stand-down.

**Hard-to-reach dimension.** Brisbane and Logan are among the most linguistically
diverse areas in Queensland. A first-in-50-years event means there is no community
memory, no elder who has done this before, and no informal knowledge network to fall
back on when the official message is unclear or in the wrong language.

**Lesson.** Directly relevant to a Queensland-framed pitch. Preparedness messaging that
assumes prior experience of a hazard fails for newcomers *and*, for novel hazards, for
almost everyone. And a slow-onset, high-uncertainty threat is the hardest case for
maintaining credibility across multiple days.

### 5. COVID-19 multicultural messaging — Australia, 2020–21 **[peer-reviewed]**

**What happened.** Rapidly changing public health rules had to reach every community in
the country, at speed, repeatedly.

**What failed and what worked.**
- Translated public health materials were repeatedly criticised for **inaccurate,
  incoherent, or garbled translations** — mistranslation at scale, produced fast, with
  no community verification loop.
- What did work: **community leaders and other information intermediaries** acted as the
  effective last mile — reinterpreting, contextualising, and vouching for official
  information within their own networks.

**Lesson.** The most important finding in this whole file. When institutional trust is
low, the *messenger* determines whether a message is believed, more than the message
does. This is the "believe" step of the chain, and no amount of translation quality
substitutes for it. It also shows the failure mode of pure automation: fast, wrong
translation is worse than slow, right translation, because it burns credibility.

## International cases

### 6. Lahaina wildfire — Maui, Hawai'i, 8 August 2023 **[inquiry]**

**What happened.** Wind-driven fire destroyed Lahaina. **102 deaths** — the deadliest
US wildfire in over a century.

**What failed.**
- **The siren network was never activated.** The emergency management director stated
  he feared people would read sirens as a tsunami warning and flee *inland, toward the
  fire*. He resigned days later. A signal so ambiguous it can't be used in an emergency
  is not a warning system.
- A cellphone Immediate Evacuation Warning was sent at 4:16 pm — **only part of the town
  received it.**
- **Power and cellular service failed**, removing the channels the plan depended on.
- The Maui after-action report explicitly names the difficulty of communicating
  evacuation "**to a transient tourist population that speaks multiple languages**".
- Loss of situational awareness inside the response: staff were unclear on the fire's
  extent and unaware the Lahaina fire had **reignited**.
- Fire crossed the Lahaina Bypass around 3:20 pm, closing a main evacuation route;
  people were directed into roads that no longer led out.
- Identified failure set: evacuation route mismanagement, inadequate warning activation,
  misinformation about containment, unsafe shelter-in-place advice, poor coordination.

**Hard-to-reach dimension.** Lahaina's population included tourists, seasonal and
hospitality workers, and Native Hawaiian families — three groups with completely
different languages, channels, and levels of local knowledge, in the same few streets.

**Lesson.** Every channel in the plan (siren, cell, power, road) failed or was withheld,
and there was no fallback. Also: a single undifferentiated alarm tone carries no
meaning, so it can't be used when meaning matters. Multi-hazard signals need to be
distinguishable, or they become unusable exactly when needed.

### 7. DANA floods — Valencia, Spain, 29 October 2024 **[inquiry / judicial]**

**What happened.** An extreme cut-off low produced catastrophic flash flooding around
Valencia. **223 deaths**, ~15,000 displaced, damages estimated in the tens of billions
of euros.

**What failed.**
- The national met agency (AEMET) had issued its highest-level warning that morning.
  Meteorologists knew the Poyo ravine situation was critical **hours before** the public
  was told.
- The regional government did not convene its emergency coordination centre (CECOPI)
  until **5 pm**, and the **ES-Alert mass phone alert was not sent until after 8 pm** —
  by which time many areas were already underwater and people were already dead.
- Spanish courts opened a criminal investigation, with a judge citing "**clear
  inaction**" and focusing on the delay.
- **Low public risk perception** was identified as a decisive co-factor: people did not
  interpret the conditions as life-threatening even as they developed.
- Damning contrast: the **Japanese embassy in Spain, acting on the same public AEMET
  data, warned its nationals a day in advance.**

**Lesson.** The single most useful case for this brief. Every technical component
worked. The forecast was right, the alert infrastructure existed and functioned, and
223 people still died — because of a decision-and-authority gap between the body that
knew and the body allowed to press the button. Alerting is an *institutional* problem
as much as a technical one. And the embassy contrast proves it: the same data, read by
a different actor, produced a warning a full day earlier.

### 8. Kahramanmaraş earthquakes — Türkiye and Syria, 6 February 2023 **[inquiry]**

**What happened.** Two M7.8 / M7.5 earthquakes in winter, hours apart, across a region
of ~14 million people. More than 55,000 deaths. INSARAG's largest-ever international
search and rescue operation.

**What failed.**
- **The first 72 hours.** Official after-action review focused on breakdowns in
  coordination and information flow in exactly the window where survival probability in
  rubble is highest.
- At least **50% of major health centres were damaged**, and only about **30% of
  pre-existing doctors** were able to help. Shortages of trauma kits, serum, and
  painkillers; heavy machinery urgently needed and slow to arrive.
- International SAR teams arrived operationally ready but hit logistics and
  coordination gaps, and were unprepared for winter conditions and sustained aftershocks.
- CDAC Network's six-month review found affected people **still lacked actionable
  information** on how to access services, and faced physical barriers to information
  access.

**Hard-to-reach dimension.** Southern Türkiye hosted a very large Syrian refugee
population — Arabic-speaking, often undocumented, frequently in the worst-quality
housing, and with strong reasons to avoid contact with authorities. Cross-border
northwest Syria was harder still, with contested access and near-absent formal alerting.

**Lesson.** At scale, the binding constraint stops being "is there a message" and
becomes "can anyone find out where help is and how to reach it". Post-impact
**service-access information** is a distinct, badly-served information need, separate
from pre-impact warning — and it is where undocumented and refugee populations drop out
of the system entirely.

### 9. Great East Japan Earthquake and tsunami — 11 March 2011 **[peer-reviewed]**

**What happened.** M9.0 earthquake and tsunami; approximately 18,500 dead or missing.

**What failed.**
- Warnings issued before 3.11 were largely **informational** — arrival time, wave height
  — with **little instruction on what people should actually do**. Post-3.11 warnings
  were deliberately rewritten into an **imperative, directive** register ("evacuate now",
  not "3 m wave expected").
- The initial tsunami height estimate was too low, and people who heard it made
  decisions on that basis.
- Foreign residents and visitors faced acute language barriers. This was **not a new
  finding**: after the 1995 Kobe earthquake, volunteers had already had to stand up a
  24-hour multilingual hotline for residents isolated by information deprivation. The
  same gap reappeared 16 years later.

**What Japan changed afterwards** — see [`existing-solutions.md`](existing-solutions.md):
*Easy Japanese* (やさしい日本語), the multilingual Safety Tips app, and directive-register
warning templates.

**Lesson.** Two things. First, **a technically accurate warning that omits the required
action does not produce the action** — this is the "act" step failing even when receive,
understand, and believe all succeed. Second, "a number in metres" assumes the recipient
can convert a measurement into a personal risk judgement, which requires local knowledge
that newcomers do not have.

### 10. Grenfell Tower fire — London, 14 June 2017 **[inquiry]**

**What happened.** Fire spread up combustible cladding on a 24-storey residential tower.
**72 deaths.**

**What failed.**
- The **"stay put" advice**, valid only for a fire contained in one flat, was maintained
  as the building's compartmentation failed. Expert evidence to the Inquiry was that
  stay-put had **effectively failed within about 30 minutes**, but residents were not
  told to evacuate until **2:47 am — over an hour later**.
- Control-room handling showed the same failures previously identified after an earlier
  fire: the organisation had not learned.
- The Inquiry heard from a bereaved 16-year-old that proceedings were **not
  comprehensible to her non-English-speaking mother** and that interpreters were needed.

> **Accuracy note.** Grenfell's residents were a highly multilingual, largely
> migrant community, and this is widely discussed. In the sources I verified, the
> *documented* language-access finding relates to the Inquiry process, not to a
> specific finding that fire safety instructions were withheld in translation.
> **Do not assert the stronger claim in the pitch without a direct citation.**

**Lesson.** The instruction was wrong, but the deeper failure is that there was **no
mechanism to revoke it**. A standing instruction that residents have been trained to
trust becomes lethal the moment conditions invalidate it and no one can reach them to
say so. Any system that pushes an instruction needs a designed path for retracting it.

## Cross-case pattern

| Case | Message sent? | Correct? | Reached everyone? | Understood? | Actionable? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lismore 2022 | partly | partly (bad gauges) | **no** | — | — |
| Vic floods 2022 | yes | yes | **no** (English only) | **no** | **no** (coercion) |
| Lahaina 2023 | partly | partly | **no** | **no** (multilingual) | **no** (route cut) |
| Valencia 2024 | **too late** | yes | yes | yes | too late |
| Türkiye 2023 | n/a (no warning possible) | — | — | — | **no** (service info) |
| Tōhoku 2011 | yes | **understated** | mostly | **no** (foreigners) | **no** (no action) |
| Grenfell 2017 | yes | **wrong** | yes | yes | **fatally wrong** |

Read down the columns: **the failure is almost never the sensing or the forecasting.**
It is delivery, comprehension, credibility, and the ability to act. That is the entire
argument for the problem definition, and it is worth putting this table on a slide.
